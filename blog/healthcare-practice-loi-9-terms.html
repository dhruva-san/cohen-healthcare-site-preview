Healthcare Practice LOI: The 9 Terms Every Seller Must Negotiate.

David Cohen, JDManaging Attorney, Cohen Law Firm PLLC

A letter of intent is the document that sets the tone for an entire transaction — and it's also the document most healthcare practice owners spend the least time on, because it's "non-binding" and the real negotiation supposedly happens later. That's partly true. But the LOI also establishes the framework, the exclusivity period, and often the negotiating posture for everything that follows. Getting it wrong rarely kills a deal outright — but it consistently costs sellers leverage they don't get back.

Whether you're a med spa owner, an optometrist, a veterinarian, or the CPA advising one of them, here are the nine terms inside a typical healthcare practice LOI that deserve real scrutiny before signing — even though the LOI itself is "just a starting point."

1. Purchase price mechanics

The headline number is rarely the whole story. LOIs typically specify a total enterprise value, but how that translates into actual proceeds depends on adjustments for working capital, debt, cash on the balance sheet, and sometimes inventory or accounts receivable. Two LOIs with the same headline number can result in materially different actual proceeds depending on how these mechanics are defined.

2. The breakdown between cash, earnout, and equity rollover

This is often the single most consequential term in the entire LOI, and it's frequently buried in a sentence or two. An LOI might state "$8M purchase price" when the actual structure is $5M cash at close, $2M earnout over two years, and $1M in rolled equity. Each of those three components carries completely different risk profiles — and negotiating the LOI is your opportunity to push back on a structure that's too heavily weighted toward contingent or illiquid consideration.

3. Earnout metrics and definitions

If any portion of the deal is an earnout, the LOI should at minimum signal what metric it's based on (revenue, EBITDA, patient volume) and over what time period. The specific definitions matter enormously — "EBITDA" can be defined in ways that are favorable or unfavorable to the seller depending on what add-backs and adjustments are permitted. Vague earnout language in an LOI is a flag that this will need careful attention in the definitive agreement.

4. Exclusivity period and "no-shop" provisions

Most LOIs include an exclusivity period during which the seller agrees not to negotiate with other potential buyers — typically 60-120 days. This is standard, but the length matters: a longer exclusivity period gives the buyer more time to potentially renegotiate terms downward (a practice sometimes called "re-trading") once you're committed to the process and other options have cooled.

5. Diligence scope and timeline

The LOI often outlines what the buyer expects to review during diligence and how long that process will take. A diligence period that's vague or open-ended can extend your exclusivity commitment indefinitely. Clear diligence scope and timelines protect you from an extended limbo period.

"The LOI is non-binding on price — but it's often binding on exclusivity. That asymmetry is exactly why it deserves more attention than sellers typically give it."

6. Employment / transition agreement signals

Many LOIs include a brief reference to post-close employment — something like "Seller to remain as Medical Director for a minimum of 3 years." This single sentence can represent years of your future working life, yet it's often the least-negotiated part of the LOI. Pay close attention to any language about post-close role, compensation, and duration — even if the LOI says full terms will be in the employment agreement, the framework set here matters.

7. Representations and warranties expectations

Some LOIs signal expectations around representations and warranties (statements about the practice's legal and financial condition) and indemnification (what happens if those statements turn out to be inaccurate). While full R&W and indemnification terms are negotiated in the purchase agreement, an LOI that signals unusually broad expectations here is worth flagging early.

8. Escrow and holdback terms

It's common for a portion of the purchase price to be held in escrow for some period post-closing, to cover potential indemnification claims. The LOI may specify a percentage and duration. Escrow terms that are unusually large or long-lasting effectively delay part of your proceeds — worth understanding before you're deep into exclusivity.

9. Conditions to closing

Beyond standard diligence, LOIs sometimes include specific conditions — financing contingencies for the buyer, regulatory approvals, third-party consents (like landlord approval for lease assignment), or specific personnel remaining with the practice. Each condition is a way the deal could fail to close even after months of work, and understanding them upfront helps you assess how solid the offer really is.

The bottom line

An LOI is "non-binding" in the sense that the final numbers can still move — but it's binding in the sense that it sets expectations, locks you into exclusivity, and establishes the negotiating frame for everything that follows. Spending a few hours with independent counsel reviewing these nine areas before signing costs little and can meaningfully change your position for the rest of the transaction.

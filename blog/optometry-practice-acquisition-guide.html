Optometry Practice Acquisition: What Every OD Should Know Before Signing.

David Cohen, JDManaging Attorney, Cohen Law Firm PLLC

"Acquisition" in optometry covers two very different situations that get talked about as if they're the same thing. One is an independent OD buying an existing practice from a retiring owner — a transaction between two individual practitioners. The other is a PE-backed platform like Total Vision, Acuity, Keplr, MyEyeDoctor, or EyeCare Partners acquiring a practice as part of a larger consolidation strategy.

If you're on the selling side of either of these, the document you'll eventually sign looks superficially similar — a purchase agreement, transferring ownership of the practice — but the substance underneath can be very different. This article covers what matters in both, with particular attention to the PE platform scenario, since that's where we see the most value left on the table by unprepared sellers.

Independent-to-independent acquisitions: what matters most

When you're selling to another OD — someone who will personally run the practice going forward — the core issues are usually more straightforward, but still consequential:

Patient records and transition

How patient records transfer, what communication goes out to patients about the ownership change, and how long you (if you're staying on temporarily) are expected to be involved in the transition all affect both the buyer's ability to retain patients and your post-sale obligations.

Non-compete scope

If you're not fully retiring, the geographic radius and duration of any non-compete matters enormously — especially in markets where patients may be willing to travel to follow a trusted OD.

Equipment and lease

Whether equipment is owned or leased, and whether the practice location's lease can be assigned to the buyer, are practical issues that can complicate even a straightforward sale if not addressed early.

PE platform acquisitions: where it gets more complex

When the buyer is a PE-backed platform, the transaction typically includes several additional components beyond a simple purchase price — and these are the areas where independent ODs most often don't realize what they're agreeing to.

1. Purchase price structure: cash, earnout, and rollover

The headline number presented in an LOI is rarely 100% cash at closing. It's common for a meaningful portion to be structured as an earnout (contingent on future performance) or an equity rollover (reinvestment into the platform's equity). Understanding the actual breakdown — and what each component is really worth — is the single most important step before evaluating any offer.

2. Earnout metrics you won't fully control

If part of your price is an earnout based on practice revenue or patient volume over the next 1-3 years, remember: after closing, you may no longer control marketing spend, staffing, hours, or pricing — all of which affect those numbers. Negotiating realistic targets, clear definitions, and some degree of operational input during the earnout period protects the value you're counting on.

3. What "equity rollover" actually means for you

Some platforms offer (or require) rolling a portion of your proceeds into equity in the larger company. This can be genuinely valuable if the platform performs well — but it's also illiquid, and you're now a minority stakeholder in a company you don't control. Questions worth asking: what's the actual current valuation basis for this equity, when (if ever) can you sell it, and what protections do you have if the platform issues more equity later (dilution)?

"The number on the LOI and the number that ends up in your bank account at closing are often two very different numbers — and the gap is almost always explained by earnout and rollover structure that wasn't fully understood upfront."

4. Your role and agreement after closing

Most PE acquisitions of optometry practices include an employment or transition services agreement for the selling OD — often with production requirements, defined hours, and restrictions on activities outside the platform. This agreement frequently has more day-to-day impact on your life than the purchase agreement itself, yet sellers often spend far more negotiating time on price than on these terms.

5. Associate doctor agreements and staff retention

If you have associate ODs, their existing agreements — and how those transfer or get renegotiated as part of the sale — affect both the platform's valuation of your practice and your associates' futures. This is worth thinking through before the buyer's diligence team raises it.

The bottom line

Whether you're selling to a fellow OD or a national platform, the document you sign will shape years of your professional life — and in a PE transaction, the headline purchase price is often the least informative number in the entire deal. Independent counsel who has reviewed these structures before can help you see past the headline number to what the offer actually means for you.

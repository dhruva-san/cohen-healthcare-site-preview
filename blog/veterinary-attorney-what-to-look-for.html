Veterinary Attorney: What to Look for When Your Practice Is on the Line.

David Cohen, JDManaging Attorney, Cohen Law Firm PLLC

If you own a veterinary practice, there's a reasonable chance you've already had one of these conversations — a call from a broker, a letter from a regional development team, or a friend at a conference mentioning that "someone made them an offer." The veterinary consolidation wave is the most active in healthcare right now, and NVA, VCA (Mars), Thrive Pet Healthcare, Pathway Vet Alliance, and Southern Veterinary Partners are all actively building.

At some point — whether that's now, in two years, or in ten — you'll likely face a decision about your practice's future that involves a transaction. And when that moment comes, the attorney you choose to represent you will materially affect the outcome. Not in a vague "good legal advice matters" way, but in specific, dollar-denominated ways: the earnout terms you accept, the equity rollover structure you agree to, and the employment terms that govern your life for the next three to five years.

Why "my regular attorney" usually isn't the right call

Most veterinary practice owners have a relationship with a local attorney — someone who helped with a lease, maybe an employment dispute, perhaps the original practice formation. That attorney is often perfectly competent at general business law. But a PE platform acquisition isn't general business law. It's a specific, recurring transaction type with patterns that only become visible after you've seen dozens of them.

Here's what that means in practice. NVA, VCA, Thrive, and Pathway each have standard deal structures — not identical, but recognizable. Their purchase agreements use similar frameworks for earnouts, similar approaches to equity rollover, and similar employment agreement templates for the selling veterinarian. An attorney who has reviewed these specific structures before knows, going in, which provisions are genuinely fixed (don't waste time negotiating) and which are routinely negotiable (worth pushing on, every time).

A general business attorney encountering this for the first time has to figure that out from scratch — usually during your 60-90 day transaction window, with your money and your future on the line.

"The first question I ask any vet who calls about an LOI isn't 'what's the purchase price' — it's 'who's the buyer, and what does their standard paper look like.' That tells me almost everything about where the real negotiation will happen."

What a transaction-experienced attorney actually does differently

The difference shows up in four specific areas:

1. Earnout structure and metrics

Earnouts tie part of your purchase price to future performance — typically revenue or EBITDA over one to three years post-close. The trap: once you've sold, you often no longer control the levers that drive those numbers — staffing decisions, pricing, hours, marketing spend, even which procedures are referred out versus kept in-house. An experienced attorney negotiates not just the earnout percentage, but the definitions, the protections, and your operational authority during the earnout period.

2. Equity rollover mechanics

Many platform deals offer — or require — rolling a portion of your proceeds into equity of the larger platform. This sounds like "upside," and sometimes it is. But it also means a portion of your sale isn't cash — it's an illiquid stake in a company you don't control, with terms around when (and whether) you can ever convert it to cash. Understanding the real value and liquidity timeline of that equity is essential before you agree to roll any portion of your proceeds.

3. Post-close employment terms

This is, in our experience, the single most underestimated part of these transactions. The purchase agreement gets all the attention, but the employment agreement governs your actual day-to-day life for years afterward — production requirements, scheduling control, non-compete radius and duration, and what happens if the relationship doesn't work out. Sellers who focus exclusively on the purchase price often find themselves locked into employment terms that make the next several years far less pleasant than expected.

4. Knowing what's actually negotiable

PE platforms operate with standard paper for a reason — efficiency. But "standard" doesn't mean "non-negotiable." An attorney who has seen a platform's paper across multiple transactions knows which clauses are templates that get pushed back on routinely, and which are genuinely fixed corporate policy. Pushing hard on the wrong things wastes time and goodwill; not pushing on the right things leaves real value on the table.

Questions to ask any attorney before you hire them for this

  • Have you reviewed purchase agreements from this specific buyer (or buyers like them) before?
  • How many veterinary practice transactions have you closed in the last two years?
  • Can you walk me through how earnouts are typically structured in deals like mine?
  • Who will actually be working on my file — you, or someone more junior?
  • What's your approach to the employment agreement — do you negotiate it as carefully as the purchase agreement?

If the answers to these questions feel vague or generic, that's useful information too. The right attorney for a transaction like this should be able to talk specifics — buyer names, deal patterns, real numbers from past negotiations (anonymized, of course) — not just general reassurances about "fighting for you."

The bottom line

Your veterinary practice is very likely the largest financial asset you own, and a PE transaction is very likely the largest financial decision you'll make regarding it. The cost difference between generalist counsel and transaction-experienced counsel is typically a small fraction of the value at stake in the negotiation itself — which is precisely why it's worth getting right.

Med Spa Attorney: What to Look for Before You Hire One.

David Cohen, JDManaging Attorney, Cohen Law Firm PLLC

Med spas occupy a strange position in the legal landscape: they're businesses that look, from the outside, like any other small medical practice — but the regulatory framework underneath is genuinely unusual. Corporate practice of medicine rules, PC-MSO ownership structures, medical director supervision requirements, and anti-kickback considerations all apply in ways that vary significantly by state and by the specific services offered.

Most general business attorneys have simply never encountered this combination before. That's not a knock on them — it's just not common work. But it means that, for med spa owners, the choice of attorney matters more than it might for a typical small business. Here's what to actually look for.

1. Have they actually built a PC-MSO structure before?

This is the single most important question. A PC-MSO (professional corporation / management services organization) structure separates the clinical entity — owned by a licensed provider — from the business operations entity, which can have non-physician ownership. In most states, med spas offering medical aesthetic services need some version of this structure to comply with corporate practice of medicine rules.

An attorney who has done this before can walk you through the structure in five minutes, with specific examples. An attorney encountering it for the first time will need to research it — on your timeline, often with your business already operating under a structure that may not be compliant.

2. Do they understand medical director agreements — really?

The medical director agreement is, in our experience, the most commonly mishandled document in med spa ownership. It's not unusual to find med spas operating with a medical director agreement that's essentially boilerplate — pulled from a template, signed years ago, and never revisited as the practice's services or staffing evolved.

The problem: many of these agreements don't actually satisfy the supervision requirements of the state the practice operates in, and the compensation structure may raise anti-kickback concerns depending on how it's framed. An attorney who understands this space will ask specific questions about your state's supervision requirements, your medical director's actual involvement, and how compensation is structured — not just hand you a template.

"The question I ask every new med spa client is: 'Walk me through what your medical director actually does, day to day.' The answer almost always reveals whether the existing agreement matches reality — and it usually doesn't."

3. Can they speak to anti-kickback considerations specific to aesthetics?

Anti-kickback rules generally prohibit certain compensation arrangements between parties who can refer business to each other. In a med spa context, this can apply to medical director compensation, relationships with referring providers, and certain commission-based compensation structures for staff. An attorney who's worked in this space should be able to identify, in a conversation, whether your current compensation arrangements raise any flags — without needing to do extensive research first.

4. Do they think about transactions, even if you're not selling?

Medical spas are increasingly acquisition targets — PE-backed MSO platforms and aesthetics-focused funds are actively building portfolios. Even if a sale isn't on your radar, the way your practice is structured today affects its value and saleability later. An attorney who thinks about your entity structure, contracts, and compliance posture with an eye toward "how would this look to a buyer's diligence team" is doing you a favor whether or not you ever sell — because that same structure also protects you in the ordinary course of business.

5. Are they straightforward about what they don't know?

This sounds counterintuitive, but it's one of the most useful signals. Med spa regulation varies meaningfully by state — what's required in Texas may differ from California or Florida. An attorney who says "I've done this structure in [other states], but let me confirm the specific requirements in your state before we finalize anything" is being honest about the work involved. One who claims instant universal expertise across all 50 states' med spa regulations is probably not being fully accurate.

The bottom line

Med spa legal work isn't harder than other small-business legal work — it's just different, in ways that aren't obvious until something goes wrong (a regulatory inquiry, a buyer's diligence team flagging an issue, or a medical director relationship ending badly). Choosing an attorney who has specifically done this work before — PC-MSO structures, medical director agreements, anti-kickback-aware compensation design — is the highest-leverage decision you can make for your practice's legal foundation.
